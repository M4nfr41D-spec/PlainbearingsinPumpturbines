Preselection Guide · Calibrated Scenario Exploration · v0_1_2

Elastic or rigid? It depends on the mechanism.

Every chart now derives from a unified modal parameter set for each material: storage modulus E', loss factor tan(δ), modal damping ζ, modal mass m_eff and clearance-dependent stiffness k(c). From these follow the assumed natural frequency f₀, Q-factor and transfer function |H(f)|; the spectra and shock curves are illustrative, not derived from |H(f)|. Two sliders drive the operating point (angle, Δd); a third reveals the shock regime where compliance becomes the protective property.

What this is · and what it is not

A preselection aid for guide-vane bearings in reversible pump-turbines — built around one question: when does an elastic bearing material outperform a rigid one, and when is it the other way round?

What it does

  • Shows which damage mechanism plausibly governs a given load situation
  • Screens both materials against their datasheet hard limits — non-compensatory
  • Separates capacity questions from edge-loading, damping and shock questions
  • Names its own evidence class for every number on screen
  • Ends in the next engineering question, not in a verdict

What it does not claim

  • No service life, no wear rate, no years
  • No exact forces away from the single calibration anchor
  • No resonance prediction — f₀ is assumed, not measured
  • No material "winner" and no substitute for machine data
  • Not a turbine simulation, FE solver or CFD model
Use it to open a conversation, not to close one. The whole model rests on a single calibrated operating point (power mode · 0° closed · Δd 0.200 mm). Everything else is reconstructed, extrapolated or hypothesised — and labelled as such. Where a result looks interesting, the right next step is a measurement, not a specification.

Modal Physics Insight
tex 552 has a low Q-factor (sharp resonance is suppressed by viscoelastic damping) but sits close to fa. bm 392 has a high Q-factor (very sharp resonance peak) but f₀ sits far from fa, so the sharp peak doesn't matter. Result: tex's broad damped response and bm's narrow off-frequency response give the measured PSD ratio of 1.58. Move the clearance slider to see how f₀ — and therefore amplification — shifts for each material.
These values combine DMA-sourced material data with modal parameters assumed to fit the reference spectral ratio. f₀ is assumed, not derived from stiffness and modal mass — one ratio cannot uniquely identify two natural frequencies. Engineering Insight
Force build-up phase — bm advantage maximises here.
Between 25° and 0°, the bearing-force ramp differentiates materials most strongly. tex 552 develops higher absolute forces (compliance allows shaft deflection); bm 392 stays lower (stiffness prevents deflection). Pressure is the inverse story.
Methodology & honest disclosure

Direct measurements (from a manufacturer test report on these compound classes): contact pressure at closed position (54.4 MPa tex, 116.1 MPa bm) from FE analysis; PSD spectra at standard clearance Δd = 0.200 mm (dominant 47 Hz peak in Power-Gen mode, PSD ratio tex/bm = 1.58); bearing forces F1/F2/F3 across rotating angle.

Modal parameter foundation (v7): All frequency-domain and resonance computations derive from a single per-material parameter set — storage modulus E', loss factor tan(δ), modal damping ζ, natural frequency f₀, and Q-factor. Reference values come from DMA characterisation (50 Hz, room temperature). The SDOF transfer function |H(f)| = 1/√((1-r²)² + (2ζr)²) drives resonance amplification at every clearance. Calibration check: at Δd = 0.200 mm, |H_tex(fa)|/|H_bm(fa)| ≈ 1.62 — matches measured PSD amplitude ratio 1.58.

Modelled extrapolations (steady-state regime): Clearance dependency uses asymmetric U-curves anchored at the measurement point (tex optimum ≈ 0.15 mm, bm optimum ≈ 0.12 mm). Load-dependent scaling applies clearance impact strongly at high load for tex (compliance saturation, sensitivity = load^1.5) and uniformly for bm (sensitivity = 0.6 + 0.4·load). f₀(c) linear approximation: df₀/dΔd = −60 Hz/mm for tex, −120 Hz/mm for bm (bm's higher stiffness coefficient makes it twice as clearance-sensitive).

Modelled shock/impact response (transient regime): Single-degree-of-freedom approximation. tex 552 treated as viscoelastic low-pass filter with characteristic time τ_char = 5 ms; bm 392 treated as near-rigid with small dynamic overshoot. Peak attenuation: tex_attn = 1/√(1+(τ_char/τ)²). This swaps the material verdict for fast impulses — compliance becomes the protective property, stiffness becomes the liability.

Not modelled: multi-mode resonances (real bearings have multiple modes — bending, shear, torsional); temperature shift of material properties; squeeze-film damping at very tight clearance; misalignment edge-loading; wear-over-time clearance drift; cumulative fatigue from repeated impacts; cavitation acoustic emission damage.

For final design decisions: modal impact testing of the installed system + DMA characterisation is recommended. This tool captures the dominant physics across six operating regimes — steady-state forces, dynamic vibration, resonance positioning, shock response, and now exposes the modal parameter set that drives it all — but it is not an absolute prediction.
